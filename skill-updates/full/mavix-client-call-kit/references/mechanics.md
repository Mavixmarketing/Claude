# What 39 live sales calls and 17 sales books actually taught

Distilled from teardowns of real agency and B2B calls (Iman Gadzhi, Jordan Platten, Alex Hormozi, Jeremy Miner, Chris Do, Andy Elliott, Pavlo, Carson Reed, Hunter Ceroy, Trent Dressel, JP Middleton and others), plus Hormozi's *$100M Offers/Leads/Money Models*, Miller's *StoryBrand*, Voss's *Never Split the Difference*, Enns's *Win Without Pitching*, Ross's *Predictable Revenue*, Kazanjy's *Founding Sales*, Warrillow's *Built to Sell* and Kim & Mauborgne's *Blue Ocean Strategy*.

---

## The eleven mechanics that actually move calls

**1. The agenda frame is the most valuable 45 seconds on any call.**
Four jobs at once: sets expectations, takes control, positions you as someone who does this often, and makes "no" allowed. Every competent seller in the corpus does one. Every failed call skipped it.

**2. Permission asks before every pointed move.**
*"Can I make a suggestion?"* · *"Do you mind if I ask you something a bit personal?"* · *"Want me to show you what I found?"* Asking permission converts a pitch into something they invited.

**3. Micro-commitments every 90 seconds during the pitch.**
*"Does that make sense?"* · *"Is that the kind of thing you're after?"* Small yeses make the big yes easier, and each one is a chance for an objection to surface early instead of at the price. Hormozi's rule: **never** *"do you have any questions?"* — it gets "no" and shuts the door. Ask *"what else do you want me to show you?"*

**4. Money maths during discovery, out loud, slowly.**
*"A job's worth $X, you close Y in ten, so every ten enquiries is $Z."* This is the single strongest technique in the corpus. Every finding then converts into dollars, and the monthly fee gets compared against a number the prospect said themselves. The calls that skipped it had no answer when the price was challenged.

**5. Recap before you transition.**
Play back what they said, ask *"have I missed anything?"*, get the correction. They almost always add one more thing, and it's usually the real one.

**6. Say the price, then shut up.**
Whoever speaks first loses. The corpus is unanimous. The flinch usually comes from the seller rushing the number, not from the number.

**7. Isolate the money objection.**
*"Money aside, do you actually want to do this?"* then *"So if we can make the money work, are you in?"* Now you know whether you're solving a money problem or a belief problem. No discount ever fixes a belief problem.

**8. The yes/no/maybe pre-frame.**
Stated in the agenda, it kills "let me think about it" before it exists — and makes asking for a decision at minute 27 something they agreed to, not something you're doing to them.

**9. The audit battery.**
The most effective operator in the corpus asks a near-identical fixed checklist of *"have you got a process for X?"* questions on every call. Each "no" is logged silently and becomes a pitch block later. That checklist is the spine of a free-audit call.

**10. Ghost products — recommend something you don't sell.**
Hormozi's most useful single tactic: tell them one thing they can get cheaper or elsewhere, or one thing they don't need. Trust moves faster than any pitch achieves. For Farhan: *"you don't need to pay anyone to fix that, it's twenty minutes in your own dashboard — I'll show you."* Then everything else he says is believed.

**11. Drop the tactic the second it's named.**
When a prospect says *"I know you're trying to close me,"* the winning move is *"you're right, I'll stop — I don't want to close you, I want you to do what's right."* In the corpus this produced the biggest deal in the set. Pushing after being caught never worked once.

## The failure modes, taken from calls that lost

- **Three minutes of discovery, eighteen minutes of monologue.** The single clearest cause of a lost call.
- **Reading notes back robotically** instead of listening. *"So you mentioned you wanted 1.5 million"* — they said it ninety seconds ago.
- **Staying on the surface.** Logical, business-level questions only, never one layer into what it costs them personally. The prospect opens a door — mentions their kids, mentions being stuck on the tools — and the seller walks past it.
- **A pre-built deck forced onto a one-call close.** A prospect who is booked out three months hears a slide saying "can't keep your crews busy" and stops believing you.
- **Pitching mechanics rather than outcomes.** Nobody buys the CRM integration.
- **Price with no micro-commitment first.** You then can't tell whether the "no" was the price or the fit.
- **Pitching a price to someone who can't say yes.**
- **Talking to fill silence after the number.**

## Rejected — found in the source material, banned here

These appear in the corpus and some are taught as best practice. They are not to be scripted.

**Dishonest**
- Opening a cold call with *"sorry you broke up there"* or *"your website says you're closed"* when neither is true
- Claiming to have already built them something you haven't
- Implying familiarity with a decision maker to get past a gatekeeper
- Fabricated statistics repeated as fact across multiple videos — a "Harvard 2018 study, 400% drop" and a garbled "McKinsey 10-minute" stat. Neither traces to a real source. Never cite them.
- Strawman third parties who don't exist, used to say brutal things at one remove

**Coercive**
- Using disclosed personal information as a pressure point — a divorce, children, a fear of failure, depression
- Interrogating someone's savings or asking them to borrow
- Closing a business that has told you it can't make rent
- Guilt in any form

**Fake pressure**
- "Only two slots left" when untrue
- "This price ends tonight" when untrue
- Setup-fee waivers invented live to force a same-call decision
- Refusing to end a call when someone has said no

**Commercially wrong for Mavix**
- Guaranteeing a specific number of leads or a ranking position
- Review-gating (soliciting only happy customers for reviews) — against platform policy and exposes the client
- Discounting on the call to rescue a bad fit
- Bluffing an answer to a technical question

**The line, stated positively:** everything in the script has to be something Farhan would be comfortable with the prospect hearing a recording of six months later.

## Where the published advice contradicts itself

Worth knowing so the script isn't built on a coin flip.

- **Rapport first vs get to the point.** Split roughly evenly across sources. Resolution used here: a *researched* ice-breaker does both — it's warm and it's evidence you did the work.
- **Talk/listen ratio.** Sources give everything from 33% to 60% seller talk. One SaaS study found top cold callers talk *more* than average. Resolution: 30/70 in discovery, closer to 60/40 during the audit and plan, because that part is you showing them something.
- **One-call close vs book the next meeting.** Genuinely contested. At Mavix's price points, one call is right — nobody wants two meetings for a few hundred to a couple of thousand a month. The one exception is a 15-minute walkthrough booked from an audit (https://cal.id/mavixmarketing/strategy-call): that is the first meeting, not a second one.
- **Best time to call.** Three different answers across sources. Derive it from your own data, not theirs.

**On the statistics in this space generally:** almost every number in the published sales-advice corpus is vendor self-research, uncited folklore, or a call-recording correlation restated as a causal rule. Use the structures. Don't quote the numbers.
